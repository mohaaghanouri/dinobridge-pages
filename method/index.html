How we decide what to build

Which part of your week repeats, and which part needs you?

Every person we have worked with arrived with a different job title and the same sentence: one part of my week has become unbearable. The profession changes. The shape of the problem almost never does.

We do not ask what you want automated, because almost nobody knows. You know what hurts. Our job is to find the exact line between the work that repeats and the work that needs your judgement, and to build only on the repetitive side of it. Finding that line is the hard part. Building the tool is the easy part.

How we tell one from the other.

Repetitive work — all four must be true

  1. It repeats. More than once a week, in roughly the same shape.
  2. It follows rules. If you had to explain how you do it, you could, in sentences rather than gestures.
  3. It is easy to check. A wrong result is obviously wrong to you within seconds.
  4. A caught mistake costs little. If the tool gets it wrong and you notice, you lose a minute, nothing more.

Your judgement — any one is enough

  1. You are liable for it. Your name, licence or signature is on it.
  2. Your taste is the product. People pay you precisely because the view is yours.
  3. The relationship is the product. A patient, a student, a client, a reader.
  4. It is a real decision under uncertainty, not a lookup with extra steps.

We build up to that line and never across it. Nearly every failed AI project we have been asked to rescue failed because someone built across the line, and the person quietly stopped trusting the results.

How it works

How the consulting work runs, step by step.

  1. 01

    First: we learn your business.

    How you win work, where the money leaks, what your people spend their days on, and what you have quietly stopped believing is possible. Not a workshop, not a questionnaire. We sit in the building and ask questions.

  2. 02

    Then: we map what AI opens, and where it stops.

    One page. On one side: the services you could offer, the speed you could reach, the costs you could cut. On the other: what stays with your people, because your name, your judgement or your relationships are on it. Read back to you, and you approve or reject each line before anything is built. That page is the plan.

  3. 03

    Then: we prove the first ideas live.

    In your office, with your data, in your own accounts. Two or three ideas from the page get tested. The ones that work stay. The ones that fail cost you an afternoon and teach you something.

  4. 04

    Last: your team takes over, and we decide what is next.

    The people who will run what works learn to change the rules without us, and each system gets a named owner on your side who has already changed one. Then we look at the page together and decide whether we take on the Head of AI role for the months ahead.

When we say no.

We say no out loud, and we keep the relationship. Saying no is most of the reason clients introduce us to other owners.

There is too little of it.
Under roughly an hour a week, keeping a tool running costs more than the task itself.
Every case is different.
If no two instances look alike, there is nothing repetitive to take over. There is only skill, and skill is not a bottleneck to remove.
One mistake would be too expensive.
If a single wrong result could end a career, the tool prepares the material and does nothing else.
A simple tool already exists.
A calendar, a form, a shared spreadsheet, a template. We set that up, say so, and move on.
It is a decision, not a workload.
Some people do not need a system. They need to decide something they have been avoiding. No software fixes that.

Two kinds of experience.

Two registers, on purpose. Years of professional engineering behind us, building production AI systems that other people depend on inside large organisations — that work teaches us what survives audits, handovers and colleagues who never asked for the tool. And at Dinobridge, we sit at the table of an owner-led firm or a research group, which keeps us direct: one room, one decision-maker, no committee.

Common questions

Common questions.

Who actually does the work?

Dinobridge is a small company, and its owner, Moha Aghanoori, leads every engagement personally: the person you speak to in the first call is the person who sits at your table, sets up the tools and does the teaching. Nothing is handed to a junior. Behind that: years of professional engineering, building AI systems in production that other people depend on.

I am one person, not a company. Is this for me?

Yes, and most of the people we have worked with came this way, none of them running a company at the time. You bring a question — a decision, an idea, a company to start, something to learn, a child to teach — and you get a session or a few with someone who does this for a living, in plain language, at a fixed price agreed in writing before anything starts. The first conversation is free either way, and if AI does not help with your question, you hear that in it. The page for individuals sets out how it runs.

You have a full-time job. How does that work?

Fairly asked, and the answer is on the table before you ask it. Our owner holds a full-time engineering role building production AI, and that role is where most of the production experience behind this site comes from — it is the reason we can tell you what survives an audit rather than guessing. It also means our capacity is real and limited: we take a small number of clients at a time, and the shape of an engagement — which days, on site or on video, over how many weeks — is agreed with you in writing after the first call and before there is a contract. We will never tell you a day is available and then move it. If what you need is more than we can carry, you hear that before you spend money, not after.

Is our data safe with you?

Yes, and for regulated work it is the first thing we design, not the last. Everything we build runs in your own accounts, under your own logins, with your own keys — we are not a hosting provider and we hold no credential you cannot revoke in an afternoon. Before we see anything: a mutual NDA, a data processing agreement under Art. 28 DSGVO, and a one-page scope naming exactly which systems we get access to and for how long. For law firms there is a separate confidentiality undertaking in Textform under §43e BRAO, with express instruction on the criminal consequences under §203 StGB. No training on your content, names removed wherever data has to leave your systems, and a complete record of who did what. Where a workflow cannot meet those conditions, it does not get built. The security page sets all of this out in full.

What happens after we get in touch?

We talk for thirty minutes, free, with no preparation on your side. You describe your business; we tell you honestly where AI could change something for you, where it would not, and what it would involve. If it makes sense, you receive a written proposal with a fixed price. If it does not, you leave with a clear recommendation instead — which is roughly what happens one time in three.

Can you take the Head of AI role for us?

Yes, that is the usual shape. After the consulting work we take the role on a retainer: direction, experiments, tool decisions in writing, your AI use policy kept current, leadership briefings, and a named window every week your team can rely on. Monthly, and you can stop with one month’s notice any month. Nobody on your side needs technical knowledge: you explain the business in your own words and everything technical stays our problem.

We already have IT. What do you do that they do not?

IT keeps the systems running. We ask a different question: what could this business do with AI that it cannot do today, and which of those ideas survives a real test? Then we run the tests with your people. Most IT teams are glad someone finally does that. We work with them, not around them — and if your IT team wants to own it, better still: we spend the first weeks with them, they keep the systems, and you stop paying us sooner.

We already use ChatGPT, or we have Copilot. Why would we need you?

Good — that tells us your people are willing. It also usually means company material is sitting in a tool nobody approved, with no record of what went in. A licence is not a capability. Copilot is good at the sentence in front of you; it cannot see that your contracts arrive by email and get lost, that seven quotes need normalising before they can be compared, or that every deadline in your business lives inside a document. Those are not writing problems. We work on the shape of the work, and on the written rules for what may and may not be pasted into anything.

Could we not just hire an AI engineer?

You could, and if you can already specify the role you probably should. Most businesses at this stage cannot — and an engineer without a brief builds what they find interesting. Take the consulting work first. If the answer is “hire someone”, we will say so, help you write the job description, and be glad to lose the retainer.

Will the tool make decisions for us?

No, and that is the point of how we build. The advice stays with the partner, the valuation with the agent, the direction with the founder, the priorities with the head of department. The tool takes over the repetitive work and prepares the material. Your people decide. We have refused to build the “accept all” button more than once.

You have never worked in our field. Does this still work?

Usually, yes. A manufacturer, a logistics group, a private clinic. In every field so far the engagement has run the same way: we learn the business, we map where AI opens something new and where it must stop, we run the first experiments. What we build changes completely from field to field. How we find out has not had to — and if your field turns out to be the exception, you hear that early rather than at the end.

Our people will resist this.

Some will, and usually the ones who are right to. Resistance is almost always one of two things: fear about the job, or a previous tool that made the work worse. So we build only where the person doing the work agrees the work is repetitive, we never build anything that reports on individuals, and we refuse to build the button that takes the decision away. The team-leaders example on this site is a system that organises what a manager observed and explicitly never rates anyone — we were asked for the version that watched people, twice, and said no both times.

How would we prove the return?

We agree the numbers before we start. At the beginning we write down two or three measurements with today’s values — hours on a named task, days from A to B, the count of the thing that keeps going wrong. At the end we measure the same things again on whatever we built. If they have not moved, that goes in the write-up too. And the honest arithmetic is simple: count the task for one week, multiply by what an hour of that person’s time is worth to the business, then halve it. If that number is small, we will tell you this is not worth buying.

Are we locked in to you?

No, and it is designed out rather than promised. Everything runs in your accounts, under your logins, with your API keys billed to you at cost. The code sits in your repository. Each system has a written handover and a named owner on your team who has already changed a rule in it. Nothing needs a licence, account, server or subscription of ours. If we disappeared tomorrow it would all keep running — put that in the contract and we will sign it.

What if we want to stop?

The retainer ends with one month’s notice, any month, without a reason. Everything we built keeps running: your accounts, your logins, your repository, your keys. You get the handover for each system and, on request, a full export of the logs and written confirmation that we hold nothing of yours. The consulting work has a natural stopping point built into it — at the end you decide whether anything continues, and you keep the plan and the working systems either way.

What if AI is the wrong answer for us?

Then you hear it in the first conversation, before you have committed to anything. It happens often: what looked like an AI problem turns out to be a process nobody owns, or a tool your team already has and does not use. We say so, and we say it early. If we only work it out once we are inside the business, we say it then, we stop, and we bill only for the days already spent.

Do we have to be in Berlin?

No. The first conversation is a call either way, and the Head of AI role runs partly over video once we know your business. Clients so far include Leipzig and Brandenburg. Berlin simply makes the on-site part easier to do in person.

How much of our time does this take?

Most of it is in the consulting work: time in the building with us, plus an afternoon of training for the people who will own what we build. After that, the Head of AI role costs your leadership a short meeting a week and your team the occasional hour to try something new. What we put in place takes seconds to use: ninety seconds of dictation after a site visit, one photograph of an invoice.
