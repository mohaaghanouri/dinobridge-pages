Asset managers

Every Monday, the investment team knows what the leading AI labs did, and what it means for its holdings.

A private asset manager holds listed companies that the AI labs’ work lands on — chips, memory, data centres, power — and needs to know when a release changes a thesis. Nobody on the team has time to follow the labs. Wire does it for them, and puts a short, checked briefing in front of the team before the week starts.

What the labs put out is scattered: announcements, research papers, job listings and the posts of the people inside them. In an ordinary week that is a few hundred items, most of them noise. Without help, the few that matter reach the team days late and second-hand, sometimes with the number changed on the way.

A second edition, written from the same facts, tells the firm’s own AI team what is worth trying this week.

What you get.

The watch list

The labs, the people inside them and every source, kept by your team in one place. Each person carries one sentence on why they are followed. New names that keep appearing are suggested, and a person on your team accepts or rejects each one.

The weekly reading

Early every Monday, Wire gathers everything the labs published and sets the noise aside. It reads what remains, goes back to the original source where something is missing, and decides what is a story. Deciding what matters gets the most care, because that is where a wrong call would cost the reader.

The check

Every quote, number and reference is checked against the source it cites, before a story is kept and again before the briefing goes out. A number the source does not contain fails, and nothing that fails is published. A separate reviewer then reads both editions against a written checklist.

Two editions, one set of facts

Both are written from the same checked stories, so the two teams never work from different facts. The investment edition connects a lab’s news to the listed companies it lands on, and says what would prove the reasoning wrong. The AI edition says what is worth testing, how to test it in a week, and what “no” looks like.

The briefing site

The editions, the watch list, and a record of everything set aside and why. Access is by invitation only. Readers mark what was useful; a curator also edits the watch list and can bring back anything set aside by mistake.

Safeguards you can rely on.

Every number traces to its source, and every investment decision stays with your team.

Every number and every quote in a briefing points to the item it came from, and a fixed rule enforces it, not the system’s opinion of its own work. In testing, twenty deliberately false stories were planted, and all twenty were refused. A holding is always shown as a dated disclosure, never as a live position.

The investment edition says what a development might mean for a thesis, and what would prove that wrong. It never says buy or sell and never sizes a position: it is not advice. Both editions are for the firm’s own teams only, not for its clients and not for marketing.

Before you decide.

What we need from you

One person on your side who looks after the watch list: approving suggested names and sources, and bringing back anything set aside by mistake. And the time to read the two briefings each week.

When we advise against it

We advise against a system that recommends trades or sizes positions. That is the firm’s judgement and its regulatory responsibility, and a machine that sounds sure about a position is the most expensive thing we could build. The same goes for a fluent weekly summary with no sources behind it: it reads well, and nobody can act on it.
