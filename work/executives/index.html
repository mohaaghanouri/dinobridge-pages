Executives

Nothing the company agreed to sits only in somebody’s memory.

The founder-CEO of a company of about 90 people. Not a corporate chief executive with a chief of staff and two assistants — someone who still personally answers investors, still gets pulled into hiring, and whose week has been taken over by other people’s needs. The complaint he arrived with was “I have no time to think”, which in our experience is the only complaint CEOs actually have.

The real damage is not the lost hours, it is what the hours cost: every decision gets made in the eleven minutes between two meetings, on whatever information happened to be in the room. Meetings entered cold. Commitments made out loud and remembered by nobody. Board preparation eating a weekend a quarter. Three hundred emails a day of which twelve matter, so the twelve are found late. None of that is a paperwork problem — it is a company being run on whatever its founder can hold in his head at the time.

What moves, and what does not.

What the system takes over

  • Holding the state of every relationship the company runs on: what was last agreed, what is still open, what was promised and by when
  • Making sure nothing that needs the founder reaches him late, and nothing that does not reaches him at all — with the reason attached either way
  • Watching the company’s own numbers for what changed, against what was expected, rather than reporting where things stand
  • Assembling the board pack from those same sources, in the fixed structure

What we build

The commitment record

Everything the company agreed to, to whom, by when — gathered from the conversations where it was actually agreed rather than from anyone remembering to write it down. Founders make a great many promises out loud and remember perhaps half, and the half they forget is the half that damages a relationship.

The state of every relationship

The most valuable thing on this list. Before any meeting: what was last agreed, the status of it, what is still open, the likely ask, the relevant numbers. Not a summary of a thread — the standing state of that relationship, kept current whether or not anyone asked. Walking into a meeting knowing what was promised last time changes what happens in it. This alone justified the engagement.

The change watch

The company’s own numbers, watched for what moved, by how much, against what was expected. Not another screen of charts. A CEO does not need another screen of charts; they need something that has already noticed.

What reaches you, and why

The company generates far more that wants the founder’s attention than the founder has attention. So something decides, and shows its reasoning: nothing is deleted, nothing is auto-replied, nothing is hidden, and what was set aside stays visible and open to review. A CEO who suspects the system is hiding something will check everything, and then it is worth less than nothing.

The outside watch

Named competitors, the market, and the regulatory movement that will reach your field before it reaches your inbox. This is the work every founder intends to do and none of them has an afternoon for.

Board pack assembly

Fixed structure, current numbers, last quarter’s commitments and their status. A weekend becomes a morning — and the morning goes into the narrative, the part that used to get an hour at the end.

Where it stops

It never decides and it never speaks as you.

Read more

No external communication is generated in your voice — not to investors, not to staff, not publicly. A CEO whose words can be generated has a problem that is hard to reverse: the moment it becomes known internally, every message they send is read with a question attached.

It never recommends a course of action on anything strategic. It assembles what is known, marks what is not known, and stops. The temptation to build an AI advisor for a lonely founder is strong, and what comes out is confident, plausible and cut off from anything the system could actually know about the company. And nothing is sent automatically from that mailbox. Everything waits for you.

How you see it working

Commitments kept, rather than discovered later by the person you made them to. Meetings entered knowing what was last agreed. Hours of board preparation. And the one that was actually asked for at the start: hours per week of uninterrupted thinking time, which you can count in a calendar and which was near zero.

What it asks of you, and when we say no.

What it asks of you

Fifteen minutes each morning, for the first month, checking what the system decided did and did not need you. After that, nothing.

When we say no

Any version of an AI advisory board, strategy generator or decision recommender. Also anything that generates the CEO’s voice for external use. Both were asked for. Both refused.
