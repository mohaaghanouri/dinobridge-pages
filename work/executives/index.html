Executives

From no thinking time at all to about ten hours a week.

The founder-CEO of a company of about 90 people. Not a corporate chief executive with a chief of staff and two assistants — someone who still personally answers investors, still gets pulled into hiring, and whose week has been taken over by other people’s needs. The complaint he arrived with was “I have no time to think”, which in our experience is the only complaint CEOs actually have.

Read more

The real damage is not the lost hours, it is what the hours cost: every decision gets made in the eleven minutes between two meetings, on whatever information happened to be in the room. Meetings entered cold. Commitments made out loud and remembered by nobody.

Board preparation eats a weekend a quarter. Three hundred emails a day, of which twelve matter, so the twelve are found late. None of that is paperwork — it is a company being run on whatever its founder can hold in his head at the time.

The result.

The measure that was asked for at the start: hours a week of uninterrupted thinking time, counted in the calendar, from near zero to about ten. Commitments kept, rather than discovered later by the person you made them to. Meetings entered knowing what was last agreed, and board preparation in a morning instead of a weekend.

What clients say.

Ninety people, three hundred emails a day, twelve of which matter, and no hour anywhere that belonged to me. The pre-meeting brief is ninety seconds of reading and it changed what happens in the meetings, because I now walk in knowing what I promised last time. He would not build anything that writes in my voice for external use and he explained what happens internally once people suspect it, which I had not thought through. Nothing auto-sends from my mailbox. I went from effectively zero hours a week of thinking time to about ten.

The routine goes. Your judgement stays.

What we take off your desk

  • Holding the state of every relationship the company runs on: what was last agreed, what is still open, what was promised and by when
  • Making sure nothing that needs the founder reaches him late, and nothing that does not reaches him at all — with the reason attached either way
  • Watching the company’s own numbers for what changed, against what was expected, rather than reporting where things stand
  • Assembling the board pack from those same sources, in the fixed structure
  • Watching named competitors, the market and relevant regulatory movement, continuously
  • Having the material for a decision together before the meeting rather than after it

What you get.

Every promise kept

Everything the company agreed to, to whom and by when — gathered from the conversations where it was agreed, not from anyone remembering to write it down. Founders make a great many promises out loud and remember perhaps half. The half they forget is the half that damages a relationship.

Prepared for every meeting

Before any meeting: what was last agreed, where it stands, what is still open, the likely ask and the relevant numbers, kept current whether or not anyone asked. Walking in knowing what was promised last time changes what happens in the meeting. Of everything here, this alone justified the engagement.

What changed, already noticed

The company’s own numbers, watched for what moved, by how much and against what was expected. A CEO does not need another screen of charts; they need something that has already noticed.

What reaches you, and why

The company produces far more that wants the founder’s attention than the founder has attention, so something sorts it and shows its reasoning. Nothing is deleted, auto-replied or hidden, and what was set aside stays visible and open to review. A CEO who suspects something is being hidden will check everything, and then the help is worth less than nothing.

The outside watch

Named competitors, the market, and the regulatory movement that will reach your field before it reaches your inbox. This is the work every founder intends to do and none of them has an afternoon for.

Board preparation in a morning

Fixed structure, current numbers, last quarter’s commitments and their status. A weekend becomes a morning — and the morning goes into the narrative, the part that used to get an hour at the end.

Safeguards you can rely on.

Every decision, and every word in your name, stays yours.

Nothing is written in your voice for the outside world — not to investors, not to staff, not publicly. A CEO whose words can be generated has a problem that is hard to reverse: the moment it becomes known internally, every message they send is read with a question attached.

It never recommends a course of action on anything strategic. It gathers what is known, marks what is not, and stops there. An AI advisor for a lonely founder is tempting to build, and what comes out is confident, plausible and cut off from anything it could actually know about the company.

And nothing leaves that mailbox on its own. Everything waits for you.

Before you decide.

What we need from you

Fifteen minutes each morning, for the first month, checking what the system decided did and did not need you. After that, nothing.

When we advise against it

We advise against any kind of AI advisory board, strategy generator or decision recommender, and against anything that writes in the CEO’s voice for the outside world. Both were asked for here, and we declined both.
