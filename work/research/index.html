Research

Two professors went from almost no research time to eight to twelve hours a week.

Professors are hired for their research, and most of them do it on Sundays, if at all. We took the repeating work off their desks — the proposal text rewritten every year, the records nobody has time to keep — so the week has room for research again. Doctoral candidates got the same: hundreds of papers narrowed to the few that matter.

The professor’s week: a teaching load of nine hours, between four and fifteen doctoral candidates, an exam board, an appointment committee and the faculty council, six to ten unpaid manuscript reviews a year, and two or three grant proposals. About sixty per cent of every proposal already existed in earlier proposals. It was rewritten anyway, over five weeks of evenings and weekends.

The doctoral candidate’s: year three of a contract that ends in year four, 900 unread papers, three chapters that no longer agree with each other, and figures nobody can trace back to the data that made them.

The result.

Hours a week for research: from close to zero to between eight and twelve, for both professors. A proposal now takes about ten working days from the call instead of five weeks. Every supervision meeting has a written record, and every figure can be made again exactly.

What we take off your desk

  • Finding what is new in your field and sorting it by how much it matters to your question
  • Tidying the reference library: duplicates removed, details corrected, every reference checked against its official record
  • Reformatting a publication list for the DFG, the ERC, the NSF, Europass or a university’s own format
  • Keeping the reusable proposal text current and fitting it to each call’s structure and page limits
  • Tracking calls, interim and final reports, review commitments and exam dates
  • Keeping the supervision record for each doctoral candidate
  • Checking appointment applications for completeness against the published criteria
  • Recording which data and which analysis produced each figure

What stays with you

  • The research question. The hypothesis. The interpretation
  • Whether a result means anything
  • The assessment of a person — in a reference, a viva, an appointment procedure
  • What to review, and what a review concludes

What you get.

The proposal library

Everything a proposal reuses, kept current in one place: CVs in every format, the publication list, the group description, preliminary work, past results and the standard statements. About two weeks to build, mostly from three old proposals. After that, those pages are never written from nothing again.

The proposal draft

Give it a call’s headings and page limits, and the reusable material is placed where it belongs, at about the right length. Every section that needs original thought is left empty and labelled. Five weeks becomes about ten days, and nearly all of them go into the work programme — the part that gets funded.

The supervision record

One private file per doctoral candidate: what was agreed, what happened, milestones and obstacles. Ninety seconds of dictation after each meeting. If we could set up only one thing, it would be this: meetings start where the last one ended, references are written from the record, and a candidate in quiet trouble shows up four months sooner.

The literature screen

Six hundred abstracts checked against inclusion criteria you write down first. Every decision is recorded with its reason, and every borderline case comes back to you. For systematic reviews it follows the PRISMA standard and gives you the flow-diagram numbers along the way.

The figure record

For every figure: which data, which analysis and which settings produced it, so it can be made again exactly. An afternoon to set up in year one, and worth a month in year four.

Safeguards you can rely on.

Every reference is real, and every paper you cite is one you have read.

References come only from your own reference library and are checked against each paper’s official record. Nothing that writes text is ever allowed to supply a reference: invented references are the most common and most damaging AI failure in research, and at 23:00 they look exactly right. The screen gives you titles, abstracts and links, never a summary offered in place of reading.

The thinking stays yours: the argument, the hypothesis, the interpretation. Manuscripts under confidential review never go to an outside service, no data is invented, and raw data stays raw. Candidates in an appointment procedure are never ranked — the procedure is legally reviewable and subject to the AGG, and we have declined it twice.

Every engagement ends with a one-page record you keep: which tasks used AI, and which funder and publisher disclosure rules apply.

Before you decide.

What we need from you

Two weeks, once, to gather the reusable text from old proposals. Ninety seconds of dictation after each supervision meeting. And reading the forty papers the screen finds, which was always the job.

When we advise against it

We advise against a paper generator. We were asked twice — for “a first draft of the introduction” and “just the related work section” — and that section is where you show you have read the field. We also decline per-person publication metrics, which are surveillance, and when a doctoral candidate really needs a supervisor, we say so.
